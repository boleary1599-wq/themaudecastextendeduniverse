The Story So Far: The Maudecast Universe

The Story So Far

Brothers Brendan and Nathan establish the Maude Flanders Internet Museum, Theme Park and Database.

Nathan, staff member number one, brings longstanding specialist knowledge and a collection of Maude appearances; Brendan organises their research.

Their mission is to examine her speaking appearances and reconstruct her life between them. They envisage an expanding team, a potentially fifty-episode or longer programme, and a commercial strategy. Maude Art begins with Michael Baker's tattooed Maude T-shirt, awarded ten Maudes out of ten. The World Without Maude begins with France, and the brothers establish their improvised closing-song tradition.

This Episode

The Museum's unexpected acquisition

Brendan and Nathan work virtually full-time at the Maude Flanders Internet Museum, Theme Park and Database. Brendan has only recently joined the establishment, whereas Nathan has worked there for several years. Although the Museum is temporarily closed, a delivery arrives on Thursday containing new pens that appear to wobble without being shaken, alongside a collection of ancient Egyptian Simpsons memorabilia. The most substantial acquisition is a life-sized Sphinx bearing Maude Flanders' face. It stands in the Museum car park. Nathan has visited the premises despite the closure, unable to stay away from the institution. The Sphinx is too conspicuous to ignore, but the curators decide to leave it where it is. The Museum car park is consequently closed for Sphinx-related reasons.

The emergence of a rival

One week after the inaugural episode, the Museum begins receiving correspondence. A friend announces a rival podcast dedicated to Flawed Manders, Maude Flanders' evil twin. Flawed has apparently married a man whose surname is Manders. Keith, another correspondent, asks about Maude's favourite colour, a question that remains officially unanswered. The brothers' mother manages to listen to approximately half of the inaugural episode before becoming bored. Nevertheless, she is the only person to share it with others on Facebook. The curators propose maintaining a permanent register of everything they discover about Maude. They also consider the possibility that an asteroid might abruptly terminate their research during Episode 32, leaving their recordings to be discovered a million years later.

The missing action figure

The Museum examines a homemade Maude Flanders action figure created by Pablo JC, a collector whose account was last active in 2014. The figure has been fashioned from an Edna Krabappel figure and includes a Holy Rollers bowling bag, a Springfield Mall shopping bag, photographs and a Bible. Nathan owns a Britney Spears Simpsons action figure purchased for under £10 during quarantine. He considers creating his own Maude figure using Britney Spears' head, with the hair removed and remodelled, Helen Lovejoy's skirt and Miss Hoover's torso. The expense of purchasing duplicate figures threatens the project's feasibility. Pablo JC's creation receives individual ratings of 6.5 and 9 Maudes out of 10. Nathan expresses an interest in acquiring the figure for £20, alongside other customised Simpsons characters.

Spectral Speculations

The Story So Far

Maude grows up in a strict Christian household and secretly watches a donkey puppet programme at her childhood friend Janice Hubler's home.

She marries Ned Flanders and becomes mother to Rod and Todd. The family lives next door to Homer and Marge Simpson, whose domestic life contrasts with theirs.

Maude is a devout Christian and homemaker with an interest in moral campaigns. Her death occurs in Alone Again, Natura-Diddily. In the first World Without Maude speculation, she would stay home and pray rather than attend French May Day demonstrations during pandemic restrictions.

This Episode

The miraculous birth of Maude Goodman

Maude Goodman is born in 1954 in a small farming community in the Fargo area of North Dakota. Her parents have previously believed that they might be unable to have children. Maude's conception and birth are regarded as something approaching a miracle, inspiring her parents' religious devotion and establishing the spiritual foundations of her upbringing. Her mother's name is Marilyn Goodman. Maude is delivered naturally at home following an uncomplicated labour. A midwife lifts the newborn into the air and smacks her little yellow bottom. Outside, the weather is grey but pleasant. It is not raining. The Goodmans live within a farming community, although they do not necessarily own or operate a farm themselves. They are believed to remain in the area throughout their lives.

The forbidden television

By the age of seven, Maude is living under the strict religious rules established by her parents. She secretly watches television at the home of her friend Janice Hubler, developing an affection for an antiquated children's puppet programme featuring a donkey.

The domestic artisan

As an adult, Maude becomes an accomplished maker of club sandwiches and homemade wind chimes. Her sandwiches are carefully prepared, cut into triangles and secured with cocktail sticks. Her wind chimes are sufficiently valued by Ned that he offers one as a potential stake in his competition with Homer Simpson.

The guardian of domestic peace

Maude and Ned sleep in a four-poster bed. When Ned becomes restless at night following his disagreement with Homer, Maude expresses concern for his emotional wellbeing. The Flanders family publicly holds hands and prays at the miniature golf course. Their prayer concerns the safety of the competitors rather than victory. Maude is disturbed by the public confrontation between Ned and Homer, whose competitive behaviour threatens the peaceful atmosphere she values.

The lime-green Sunday dress

Maude owns a lime-green Sunday outfit with a lilac hat, a small ruff around the neck and pearl-like earrings. Ned wears the outfit while mowing his lawn as a consequence of his wager with Homer. The dress may represent an earlier period of Maude's fashion tastes. Following Ned's use of it, Maude is likely to discard the outfit and purchase a replacement.

The Literary Essay

The Terrible Burden of Being Good

Maude Flanders enters the cultural imagination carrying a plate of club sandwiches. There are little sticks holding them together. This is important. A hastily assembled ham sandwich would have communicated hospitality perfectly adequately, but the club sandwich represents an expenditure of effort beyond the immediate requirements of the situation. It is domestic labour transformed into a small exhibition of moral superiority.

In Dead Putting Society, Homer encounters the unbearable spectacle of another man's happiness. Even the relative altitude of their wives' buttocks becomes evidence of his inadequacy. The Flanders household is the suburban fantasy that constantly eludes the likes of George and Mildred or Hyacinth Bucket: an apparently effortless demonstration of having got everything right.

Maude's sandwiches are the edible equivalent of Ned's rumpus room.

Yet her concern for Ned's nocturnal restlessness, contrasted with Marge's irritation at Homer's, suggests something beyond competitive domesticity. Maude is genuinely attentive. The uncomfortable question is whether this attentiveness constitutes an independent personality or whether it is precisely what prevents one from emerging.

The woman who stared into space

The miniature golf tournament offers an extraordinary opportunity to examine Maude's emotional life. As the competitive fever intensifies, she remains almost motionless. Her arms hang by her sides, her fingers extended. Occasionally her expression changes, but her face remains largely impenetrable.

The obvious explanation is economical animation. Nobody watching The Family-Ness would ordinarily expect the facial expressions of peripheral characters to sustain detailed psychological investigation. But the distinction between cheap animation and emotional repression becomes wonderfully porous when the finished image is examined independently of its production.

Maude's stillness possesses something of the unsettling quality of the suburban women in The Stepford Wives. The smile, the appropriate clothing and the domestic competence are all present, but the machinery underneath remains inaccessible.

The most intriguing moment arrives when Bart and Todd abandon the competition and agree to a draw. Maude, apparently the principal advocate of peace, ought to experience this as a triumph. Instead, she stares into space.

What if the achievement of peace constitutes a crisis for the peacemaker?

There is something of Rimmer in Red Dwarf here: the individual whose sense of purpose depends upon the continued existence of a problem. Maude's identity may depend upon the management of other people's disturbances. The children's independent resolution temporarily deprives her of that function.

Her thousand-yard stare becomes a moment of existential redundancy.

When Homer and Ned threaten to reignite their dispute, concern returns to her face. The world has become troublesome again. Maude knows what to do with a troublesome world.

Some kind of miracle

The birth of Maude Goodman in North Dakota in 1954 gives this predicament a theological dimension. Her parents, previously doubtful that they could have children, interpret her arrival as a miracle.

She does not merely inherit Christianity. Christianity is the consequence of her arrival.

It is an origin story with something of the supernatural portent of The Omen, albeit with considerably less dramatic weather. There are no thunderclaps, sinister dogs or hysterical nurses. Just a pleasant grey day in a farming community and a midwife smacking a little yellow bottom.

A child whose existence confirms her parents' faith carries an unusual burden. Goodness becomes less a personal achievement than the repayment of an existential debt.

Even the surname Goodman sounds like something from a particularly unsubtle episode of The Twilight Zone.

Against this background, Maude's homemade wind chimes acquire unexpected significance. Unlike sandwiches, they have no immediate domestic necessity. They are objects designed to produce incidental pleasure.

One can imagine them hanging in the garden of a character from The Good Life, although Maude lacks Barbara Good's appetite for dismantling suburban convention. Her rebellion, if it is one, is considerably quieter.

The wind chime is an instrument whose maker cannot control its performance. There is a faint echo of John Cage's interest in relinquishing control over sound. For a woman whose existence appears devoted to regulating behaviour, this is a surprisingly attractive hobby.

She constructs the conditions for something unpredictable to happen.

Some assembly required

The absence of a commercially produced Maude action figure presents a peculiar counterpart to this problem of individuality.

The World of Springfield collection reportedly found room for Stephen Hawking and Rodney Dangerfield's Larry Burns, yet failed to accommodate the woman who made those immaculate club sandwiches.

The homemade Maude fashioned from Edna Krabappel belongs to the same imaginative tradition as the customised Star Wars figures and improvised Action Man accessories that populate childhood bedrooms. The proposed alternative, incorporating Britney Spears' head, Helen Lovejoy's skirt and Miss Hoover's torso, resembles a particularly low-budget Frankenstein assembled from the contents of a branch of Toys “R” Us.

But what accessories would adequately represent Maude? A Bible? A bowling bag? A club sandwich?

The wind chime is perhaps the most revealing choice. Neither a symbol of dutiful Christianity nor an instrument of domestic service, it represents a modest territory of personal creativity.

Somewhere between the immaculate sandwich, the motionless spectator and the missing plastic figurine is a woman whose goodness has made her almost invisible.

Perhaps the most interesting thing about Maude Flanders is the noise she makes when nobody is asking her to do anything.
