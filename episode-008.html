The Museum Story

The Story So Far

The Maude Flanders Internet Museum, Theme Park and Database in Worsley Mesnes has grown from a modest television archive into a precarious empire of research, tourism, merchandise and international operations. Its curators, brothers Brendan and Nathan, oversee an institution whose ambitions consistently exceed its finances.

A life-sized Maude Sphinx, dumped into a waterway, flooded neighbouring homes and produced a £200,000 liability. Stamp, the elderly nightwatchman, demands $250,000 after becoming stranded on a roller coaster. His subsequent campaign of retaliation has involved a syrup-covered stone and repeated road accidents. Nathan's gambling debts and Brendan's part-time employment have done little to stabilise the enterprise.

The Museum maintains connections in Turkey and Côte d’Ivoire, including manager Yves Kwame, while facing competition from Flawed Manders. Its cultural activities encompass Maude Art, Face Forward Simpsons illustrations, customised figures and improvised musical performances.

Uncle Statman John monitors audience figures, recently reporting approximately 903 views. His disputes with Dutch internet critics have produced an unlikely statistical protégé. T-shirt sales at Ashton Market remain a possible source of income.

The archive continues to expand. Nathan's research notes have reached sixteen pages, interns may eventually produce entire programmes, and plans for fifty episodes and further secret expansion are under consideration. The Mauditorium now contains 144 numbered entries, including speculation about Maude's thoughts and an imagined appearance in Shakespeare's The Tempest.

Most significantly, the Museum has acquired Maude's twine-bound private diary. Its first page reveals anxieties about Todd's vegetables, an obscure incident involving Ned and flossing, and suspicions about Helen Lovejoy.

Meanwhile, corrections concerning Rod's age, Ned's handedness and overlooked appearances continue accumulating. The proposed Auntie Maude comparison feature joins the Museum's growing catalogue of activities, while the ordinary Museum visit remains postponed.

This Episode

The Flungball inheritance

The Museum's disputed origin gains another explanation: Nathan recalls winning it in a Flungball competition seven years earlier. Seventy-five employees are now said to train seventeen hours a day for the next online contest, although Nathan plans to forfeit on the morning of the event without warning them.

Judge Roswell's theatrical order

With combined liabilities reported at £300,000, the institution is offered a £5,000 reduction if it stages a one-man Maude play before Nathan's birthday. The planned production nevertheless includes auditions and a seven-and-a-half-minute wedding-gown dance by Stamp, the aggrieved nightwatchman. The judicial threat of imprisonment adds urgency without improving the plan.

Another diary page

Page 93 of Maude's twine-bound diary, dated 18 September 1997, describes an anticipated journey with Marilyn and another allegation against Helen Lovejoy: the theft of Mrs Hubbard's pickles. The date adds a point to the unresolved family chronology; the accusation remains Maude's suspicion rather than established fact.

Research and corrections

The Mauditorium rises from 144 to 150 entries. Blue eyeshadow, Maude's public conduct and the representation of attractiveness in Springfield join the investigations. A Week Too Early is proposed beside A Week Too Late; a T-shirt independently connecting The Tempest with Lost complicates a previous claim of originality. The programme closes with its established improvised musical ritual.

Spectral Speculations — Maude's Life

The Story So Far

Maude Goodman Flanders grew up in North Dakota within a deeply religious family whose complicated history includes Marilyn Goodman, Francis Goodman and the wider Goodman–Turnbull relatives. Her childhood was shaped by religious expectations, family tensions and occasional rebellion, including secretly watching television at her friend Janice's house.

At approximately nineteen, Maude married Ned Flanders against the wishes of her father, Francis, who considered Ned an unsuitable older prospect. Her mother Marilyn supported the marriage, but Francis's opposition created an estrangement associated with his eventual absence from Maude's funeral. Marilyn is believed to have died before her daughter, although the date remains uncertain.

Maude and Ned established their Springfield household and raised Rod and Todd. Jasper has been suggested as a possible former owner of their home, but no evidence confirms this. The children's ages and chronology remain unresolved.

Although associated with religious obedience and domestic respectability, Maude has demonstrated considerable independence. She enjoyed an Aerosmith performance at Moe's Tavern, became angry when Todd used inappropriate language, and subsequently questioned Ned's punishment.

During a bitterly cold church service, Maude witnessed the congregation becoming trapped behind frozen doors while Groundskeeper Willie attempted a rescue with a blowtorch. She later joined her family in singing to Homer, although her individual voice remains unconfirmed.

The family's subsequent pursuit of Homer involved a dangerous passage through a railway carriage. When Homer finally returned to church and fell asleep beside her, Maude appeared distinctly displeased.

Her recovered private diary reveals further complexity. She worries about Todd's refusal to eat vegetables, questions her own parenting, records an unclear incident involving Ned and flossing, and suspects Helen Lovejoy of an affair.

Behind her composed public appearance emerges a woman capable of anxiety, suspicion, irritation and independent judgement.

This Episode

The dated diary and Marilyn

A diary page dated 18 September 1997 places Maude in contact with her mother Marilyn and anticipating a journey. This matters because the earlier biography establishes Marilyn's support for Maude's marriage and suggests that she died before Maude, without fixing the date. The diary supplies a chronological point but does not resolve the death or the more extraordinary memories attributed to Marilyn. Its strange reference to an earlier existence as a human limb must remain part of the Museum's speculative family mythology, not a verified biographical fact.

Helen and the missing pickles

Maude's earlier diary entry suspected Helen Lovejoy of an affair. The new entry levels a different accusation: Helen has been stealing pickles from Mrs Hubbard. Maude cites Helen's smell of pickles and Jessica Lovejoy's supposed allergy as clues. The evidence reveals Maude's alertness to neighbourhood behaviour, but it does not establish that any theft occurred. Her private judgements continue to differ from the composed figure presented in public.

Blue eyeshadow and a public voice

Maude's conspicuous blue eyeshadow invites speculation about vanity, self-expression and the possibility that she simply likes the colour. In a Springfield town meeting she proposes hiring firefighters to extinguish a continuing blaze. Homer dismisses her as boring, but she continues without engaging him. Her practical proposal is ultimately overwhelmed by enthusiasm for Lyle Lanley's monorail, which she also supports. The scene shows both civic independence and the limits of that independence under the pressure of the crowd.

Valentine's Day and the children

Ned appears in a heart costume to serenade Maude with a version of Rod Stewart's Da Ya Think I'm Sexy? and offers her a pink rose. Maude's response is affectionate but open to interpretation, while her own gift remains unknown. Another appearance places her at a school production involving Rod and Todd. Their names, relative ages and chronology continue to resist simple reconstruction.

Blackberry schnapps and Ann Landers

A flashback to Ned's drinking of blackberry schnapps reveals the Flanders bedroom and his insult to the advice columnist Ann Landers. Maude reacts with particular horror, suggesting that Landers occupies an important place in her moral imagination. Photographs, the telephone's position and inconsistent evidence about the couple's sleeping sides become further domestic clues, though they do not establish a single reliable arrangement. Maude emerges as a woman with her own tastes, anxieties and authorities, not simply a reflection of Ned.
