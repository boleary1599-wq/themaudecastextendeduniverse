The Museum Story

The Story So Far

The Maude Flanders Internet Museum, Theme Park and Database in Worsley Mesnes has expanded from a television archive into a precarious enterprise of research, tourism, merchandise and international ambitions. Brendan and Nathan, brothers and curators, have acquired staff, correspondents, rivals and substantial liabilities. A life-sized Maude Sphinx, removed from the car park and dumped in a waterway, caused flooding and a reported £200,000 liability. Stamp, the elderly nightwatchman, seeks $250,000 after being stranded on a roller coaster; his campaign of retaliation has included a syrup-covered stone and repeated road accidents. Nathan's gambling debts and Brendan's return to part-time work have not resolved the finances.

The institution has connections in Turkey and Côte d’Ivoire, with Yves Kwame associated with the latter. Uncle Statman John reports audience figures and pursues online critics. The rival Flawed Manders enterprise remains part of the Museum's world. Its research programmes include Maude Art, Face Forward Simpsons illustrations, proposed figures, musical performances and a growing Mauditorium. The sixth broadcast brought the Museum's finances and Stamp's compensation claim into sharper focus, while introducing a numbered selection system for the fact register.

Literary Essay

The Theology of Keeping Quiet

There is something magnificently wrong about a family risking its collective life to make sure a neighbour goes to church. The Flanders household is ordinarily presented as Springfield's safest domestic sanctuary, an oasis of politeness, religious instruction and modest refreshments. In Homer the Heretic it becomes a mobile evangelical emergency, hurtling after Homer Simpson with the reckless determination of a getaway driver. Maude Flanders is in the car. That is the important thing.

She is not driving or delivering a sermon. She does not have an identifiable spoken line. Nevertheless, she participates in a journey that might kill her husband, her children and herself. The woman apparently designed to prevent bad things from happening is travelling directly towards a potential catastrophe. It is tempting to regard this as an animation convenience: the writers require the whole Flanders family for the joke. But it also reveals what happens when goodness becomes a form of extremism.

The good people

Ned Flanders began as an irritatingly perfect neighbour, a man whose domestic success existed to expose Homer's failures. Gradually, his Christianity became more exaggerated until it threatened to replace the rest of his personality. This gave television criticism the term Flanderization: the process by which one recognisable trait consumes a character. Maude is a stranger case. She is not obviously a complicated character reduced to a single joke. She is someone whose complexity has barely been permitted to appear. Ned becomes more religious, more anxious and more absurd; Maude stands beside him, providing the visual proof that an entire household shares his convictions.

A fanatic acting alone might be alarming. A fanatic accompanied by a smiling wife and neatly dressed children becomes a respectable suburban institution. Until the institution drives through a train. The pursuit of Homer makes the contradiction explicit: the family seeks spiritual salvation while apparently abandoning the elementary business of physical survival. Certainty about the destination renders the journey irrelevant.

A woman without a voice

Maude stands with the family while they sing about Noah's Ark. She is visibly present, but her individual singing voice cannot be distinguished. It is an almost perfect image of her television existence. She belongs to the household, shares its public identity and joins its rituals. Whether she independently enjoys any of them is uncertain. Silence invites the audience to manufacture an interior life: contentment, resentment, obedience, calculation, private terror. Every interpretation fits because the character is rarely allowed to contradict it.

This is the inverse of the unreliable narrator. Here the narrator scarcely exists. Viewers construct a personality from glances, gestures and decisions that may have been made for reasons of animation economy. The result is a strangely modern figure, someone whose identity is assembled from the interpretations other people impose upon her. Even the possibility that she practises singing privately becomes a little novel built around a missing sound.

The respectable thrill

Maude has already been seen enjoying Aerosmith at Moe's Tavern. Now she is riding in a car that might be destroyed by a train. The moments are hardly equivalent, but together they suggest pleasures that her conventional image cannot comfortably accommodate. The suburban rebel is familiar in cinema, from the frustrated domestic lives of The Graduate to the more grotesque escapes of David Lynch. Respectability becomes interesting when something underneath it threatens to erupt. Maude's rebellion is quieter: she stays with her husband. Perhaps the only acceptable form of recklessness available to her is recklessness conducted under Ned's authority.

The bread question

What does Maude think about when nothing happens? Perhaps she contemplates faith, mortality and the mysteries of human existence. Perhaps she wonders whether the house needs more bread. The second possibility is wonderfully deflating. Domestic labour rarely receives the dramatic prestige of spiritual contemplation. A woman can spend her life maintaining a household and remain almost invisible because the work appears too ordinary to be a story. The cupboard empties; bread must be purchased; the cupboard empties again. A minor practical anxiety becomes a suburban Groundhog Day, the endless administrative burden concealed behind an expression of serenity.

A face like thunder

At the end, Homer returns to church and promptly falls asleep beside Maude. Her expression is severe. She has participated in the family's campaign and survived its dangers; her reward is to become a convenient surface for the newly redeemed sinner's head. There is no speech, theological declaration or explosion of rage. Just a look. For a moment, the supporting character has an opinion that seems to belong entirely to herself. A woman can believe in salvation and still wish Homer Simpson would sit somewhere else.
