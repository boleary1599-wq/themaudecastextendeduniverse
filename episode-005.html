The Museum Story

The Story So Far

The Maude Flanders Internet Movie Database, Theme Park and Museum in Worsley Mesnes is operated by brothers King Brendan and King Nathan of the Cockney Watchdogs. Its acquisition remains complicated by two apparently competing explanations: an inheritance from their grandfather and a victory in a local competition involving drawing Maude Flanders and kicking a football against a wall. The Museum's commercial development has been hampered by unsuitable deliveries, damaged merchandise and questionable management decisions. An enormous Maude-faced Sphinx obstructed the car park until the brothers transported it on Tesco shopping trolleys and deposited it in a local waterway, inadvertently flooding nearby homes. Meanwhile, the Museum's 82-year-old nightwatchman, Stamp, became stranded on a roller coaster.

Despite these difficulties, the institution has expanded internationally, establishing operations in Turkey and Côte d'Ivoire. Its merchandise includes wobbling pens, Maude-head mops, drinking vessels and masks fashioned from plastic bags. Its attractions include a wind-chime aeroplane ride. The Museum's growing cultural influence is monitored by the brothers' uncle, Statman John, whose enthusiastic statistical bulletins accompany the development of an increasingly ambitious programme of Maude-related research, correspondence and artistic appreciation.

This Episode

The regulation of institutional broadcasting

Following the sprawling previous broadcast, the brothers introduce new regulations intended to prevent the Museum's increasingly numerous features from overwhelming its primary purpose. No more than a few preliminary segments should precede the examination of Maude Flanders herself, and the programme should ideally remain within sixty minutes. The institution's intellectual ambitions nevertheless continue to grow. Jacques Derrida and deconstruction are introduced into its methodological framework, although the immediate practical consequence is Nathan's decision to undertake the episode without any research notes whatsoever. His reasoning is that notes depend upon the continued stability of the world, a condition upon which he is unwilling to rely.

Statman John's war against the internet

Uncle Statman John delivers another statistical bulletin, announcing that the second episode has accumulated 166 views. His enthusiasm for the achievement is compromised by the discovery that somebody has registered a thumbs-down. John responds by physically attacking the internet, apparently regarding it as an entity capable of being disciplined through punching and kicking. His nephews appreciate his loyalty but acknowledge that his anger may be directed less towards their detractor than towards the general phenomenon of internet disrespect. Further details emerge concerning John's private life. He maintains a playlist of macaroni-and-cheese recipe videos, which he watches repeatedly. On one occasion, his emotional response to a macaroni-and-cheese video resulted in his setting fire to his entire kitchen. His internet viewing also encompasses motorcycle stunts and unusual activities involving cream cheese. By the end of the broadcast, John is expected to spend the following week investigating internet service providers in pursuit of the unidentified detractor.

The Museum faces prosecution

The consequences of the Sphinx removal operation have finally reached the Museum's management. Brendan has received correspondence from the local council requesting that both brothers appear before a Wigan magistrate on charges of gross littering and flooding the homes of several elderly Worsley Mesnes residents. Their decision to publicly describe the operation during the previous broadcast appears to have undermined their hopes of avoiding detection. Their original intention had been to allow the Sphinx's identifying features to erode beneath the water. Brendan has also been offered his former employment as a park cleaner and is seriously considering returning to it. The brothers briefly contemplate closing the Museum altogether, although the impending court appearance makes any straightforward withdrawal from their responsibilities increasingly unlikely. The institution's expanded title now incorporates a book depository and hotcake restaurant.

Stamp seeks compensation

A second legal crisis arrives in the form of correspondence from solicitors representing Stamp. The elderly nightwatchman is seeking $250,000 in damages for the physical and psychological trauma sustained during his prolonged confinement on the Museum's roller coaster. The duration of his ordeal is now described as six and a half days, rather than the week and a half previously reported. Nathan refuses to accept responsibility, maintaining that Stamp understood what he was signing up for. He considers the legal action a waste of the nightwatchman's time and money and appears confident that, given Stamp's advanced age, he can simply outlive the dispute.

Stamp has also left another telephone message at approximately three o'clock in the morning. He continues to mistake Brendan's telephone for Nathan's. The message appears to contain a threat involving a rubbish bin, although its precise wording remains difficult to establish. The Museum's relationship with its nightwatchman has deteriorated from an employment dispute into open hostility.

The first named international manager

The manager of the Museum's Côte d'Ivoire sister institution is identified as Yves Kwame. He has contacted Brendan, but the contents of his communication are postponed until the following broadcast in accordance with the new time restrictions. His appearance establishes the existence of an identifiable management structure beyond Worsley Mesnes and the brothers' immediate supervision.

Artistic collaboration and public accountability

The brothers' relationship with the musical group Maude Flanders develops further when the musicians respond positively to correspondence concerning the previous episode's audiovisual experiment. Permission is obtained to include the video accompanying their music. Meanwhile, a new feature entitled Stand by Your Flan is introduced. Its purpose is to examine Nathan's previous public statements concerning Maude Flanders. An investigation of his Facebook history uncovers a declaration dated 23 October 2011 describing Maude as probably the least important character in television history. Nathan is reluctant to accept responsibility for the statement.

His previously asserted figure of $4,000 in additional remuneration sought by Maggie Roswell also comes under scrutiny. Unable to identify his source, he acknowledges that the figure may have survived in his memory independently of its original context. The Museum's commitment to scholarship is consequently accompanied by a growing awareness of the unreliability of its own researchers.

Correspondence from the public

A correspondent named Gingham Poofs asks which animal Maude would become if reincarnated. A horse is initially proposed because of its gentleness, loyalty and symbolic power, before a dolphin becomes the preferred choice. Its pointed facial features, apparent friendliness and unassuming manner are considered particularly appropriate. A second, anonymous correspondent questions Nathan's mental condition and the fundamental purpose of the broadcast. Rather than disputing the criticism, the brothers agree with it and invite further clarification. The broadcast concludes with an improvised song incorporating the Flanders barbecue, pharmaceutical employment, wishbones, misspelled T-shirts and Maude's maternal grandparents. Despite the new regulations, the programme exceeds its intended duration.

Spectral Speculations

The Story So Far

Maude Goodman was born in 1954 near Fargo, North Dakota, following a conception regarded by her parents as miraculous. The circumstances prompted their conversion to a deeply religious way of life. Her childhood combined religious instruction with small acts of independence, including secretly watching television at the home of her friend Janice Hubler. By the age of fifteen, Maude had developed an interest in constructing wind chimes. Her third creation, a crucifix incorporating hanging chimes, became a gift to her parents. Maude subsequently married Ned Flanders and became the mother of Rod and Todd. Her early married life reveals a woman whose apparent contentment occasionally gives way to troubling expressions of detachment, particularly during a miniature-golf competition.

Her relationship with Homer Simpson has been complicated by his inappropriate attention towards her. During a marriage retreat, she encouraged Ned to express his feelings, annotated his Bible and demonstrated her trust by allowing herself to fall backwards into his arms. Maude's friendship with Helen Lovejoy has encouraged a more confrontational aspect of her personality. Although initially supporting Marge Simpson's campaign against cartoon violence, she subsequently condemned Marge's refusal to oppose the public exhibition of Michelangelo's David. Her growing capacity for indignation exists alongside her domestic devotion, artistic interests and increasingly complicated relationship with the Simpson family.

This Episode

The artistic barbecue invitation

The Flanders family invites its neighbours to a barbecue through an illustrated invitation featuring cartoon likenesses of Ned and Maude. The drawings suggest that Maude's artistic activities may extend beyond the construction of wind chimes. The invitation's visual presentation is attributed to Maude, while the playful names given to the food appear more characteristic of Ned. The division suggests a domestic creative partnership in which Maude supplies the illustrations and Ned provides the language. The barbecue is attended by a collection of Springfield residents, including Martin Prince's parents and several less readily identifiable individuals. Maude's physical appearance also attracts attention. Viewed from the side, her unusually elongated upper lip gives her a distinctive profile, contrasting with the rounded faces of her husband and children. Helen Lovejoy possesses a similarly pointed facial structure.

The pharmaceutical years

Ned reveals that he has spent ten years working in the pharmaceutical industry. His decision to abandon this employment in favour of establishing the Leftorium raises questions concerning the earlier financial and professional history of the Flanders household. It remains possible that Maude possessed some connection with medicine or pharmaceuticals and that this provided the circumstances under which she first encountered Ned. Her educational history remains largely unknown, however, and there is insufficient evidence to establish either her qualifications or her employment before marriage. During the barbecue, Ned ceremonially burns his tie to mark the end of his previous career. Maude responds with applause and apparent enthusiasm. She displays none of the visible anxiety that accompanied the miniature-golf competition. Her willingness to support such a considerable financial risk suggests that her trust in Ned extends beyond matters of religion and domestic routine.

The wishbone and the limits of forgiveness

Maude offers Ned and Homer a chicken wishbone, encouraging them to participate in the traditional game. Homer initially refuses, but Maude persists, insisting that the activity is enjoyable. This represents an interesting departure from her previously established relationship with recreation. Maude is not merely tolerating an amusement devised by somebody else; she is actively encouraging participation in an activity involving luck, superstition and the fulfilment of wishes. Homer's eventual participation produces a succession of increasingly unpleasant wishes directed towards Ned and his business. Maude reacts with astonishment when Homer becomes aggressive towards her husband.

Despite his earlier behaviour towards her, Maude continues to offer Homer opportunities to participate in family activities. Her surprise suggests either a persistent belief in his capacity for improvement or an inability to anticipate the full extent of his hostility. When Homer begins choking during his own laughter, Ned saves him. Maude subsequently withdraws with her husband rather than confronting Homer directly. Her behaviour contrasts with the more assertive personality she displays in Helen Lovejoy's company.

The mysterious Nell

As the Leftorium begins to fail, the Flanders family is forced to sell its possessions. Homer exploits Ned's financial difficulties, acquiring household goods and wearing items of his clothing while mocking him. Two personalised T-shirts reveal an unusual inconsistency. Ned's shirt declares his love for Maud, omitting the final letter of his wife's name. Maude's corresponding shirt declares her love for Nell. The discrepancy raises the possibility of a previously unknown individual named Nell, although the matching nature of the garments makes a simple manufacturing error an equally plausible explanation. No earlier romantic attachment can presently be established.

The family without a home

The financial collapse of the Leftorium eventually leaves the Flanders family living in its car. A welcome mat bearing the words Welcome to Flanders Country is placed outside the vehicle, preserving a symbolic boundary between the family home and the outside world. Maude's response to the catastrophe is remarkable. While Ned appears burdened by the practical consequences of their situation, she remains cheerful. Wearing an elegant dark dress, she entertains Rod and Todd, using the car's cigarette lighter to toast marshmallows. The family intends to travel to Capital City to stay with Ned's sister, although the existence of this sister is disputed. Maude's own relatives are presumed to remain in North Dakota, potentially explaining why the family has not immediately sought their assistance.

Maude's composure may represent an attempt to protect her children from the seriousness of their circumstances. Alternatively, it may reflect an almost absolute confidence in Ned's ability to resolve their difficulties. A more unsettling possibility is that Maude experiences a degree of excitement at the collapse of her familiar domestic world. The loss of possessions and security offers an unexpected escape from the predictability of her previous existence.

Putting on a happy face

Maude joins Rod and Todd in singing Put on a Happy Face. Although her individual singing voice cannot be clearly distinguished, her visible participation is significant. The song continues to be heard as Ned discusses his financial ruin with Homer. Maude's optimism exists in direct contrast to her husband's despair. Ned has even been forced to sell his Bible to Homer for seven cents. It may be the same volume in which Maude previously underlined passages during their marriage retreat, although this cannot be confirmed. The loss of the Bible represents the disappearance of one of the family's most important material expressions of religious security.

The restoration of the household

Homer eventually mobilises Springfield's left-handed population in support of the Leftorium. Maude and her children greet Ned at the top of the shopping-centre escalator, visibly delighted by the unexpected reversal of their fortunes. Among the customers are several prominent Springfield residents, including Mr Burns, whose purchase of a vehicle represents a substantial contribution to the family's recovery. This is the second occasion on which Maude has witnessed apparently generous behaviour from Burns. Her impression of him may therefore differ considerably from that of other Springfield residents.

Maude's response to the family's restored prosperity is expressed through a sequence of distinctive gestures: her hands rise towards her face before being clasped together in gratitude. Her appearance and movements closely evoke Mary Bailey in It's a Wonderful Life. Although Maude's faith in her husband appears to have been justified, the actual agent of the family's salvation is Homer Simpson. The episode consequently establishes the possibility of a genuine friendship between two households previously connected primarily through geographical proximity and mutual obligation. Maude participates in the final celebrations without visibly holding a drink. Whether she abstains from alcohol remains an open question.

The Turnbull family of Washington State

Maude's maternal ancestry is established through the history of her mother, Marilyn Turnbull. The Turnbull family lived in a small logging community in Washington State around the beginning of the twentieth century. Rather than arriving in America through European immigration, the family is believed to possess longstanding American roots, potentially including Native American ancestry. Maude's maternal grandfather, Mitchell Turnbull, lost an arm. Within weeks of this event, his wife Magdalena lost a leg. The precise circumstances of these injuries remain unknown. The couple subsequently had eight children, who provided assistance with their parents' physical disabilities in an extraordinarily literal fashion.

Marilyn spent the first seven years of her life functioning as her mother's replacement leg. Attached to Magdalena's body using thorny vines, she faced backwards so that movements of her waist could reproduce the bending of a knee. Magdalena was an enthusiastic hiker, making her daughter's responsibilities particularly demanding. The steep, wooded trails of Washington State presented considerable challenges for their unusual arrangement. One of Marilyn's siblings is identified as Rex Turnbull. He might have been required to undertake similar duties had Magdalena lost her remaining leg. Instead, in an unexplained reversal of fortune, Magdalena subsequently acquired two additional legs. The family's unconventional history establishes an extraordinary precedent for the combination of self-sacrifice, physical endurance and domestic loyalty that would later characterise Maude Goodman.

Literary Essay

The Luxury of Losing Everything

Maude Flanders, the economics of optimism and the strange pleasures of domestic catastrophe.

There is something deeply peculiar about the sight of Maude Flanders smiling from the window of a car in which she has recently been obliged to live. Her husband has lost his business, her possessions have been acquired by a malicious neighbour and her children's immediate future is uncertain. Yet Maude looks as though somebody has finally organised a holiday.

The obvious explanation is Christian fortitude. Maude possesses the kind of faith that enables material catastrophe to be interpreted as a temporary inconvenience in a considerably larger spiritual arrangement. Her husband will provide, God will intervene and the family will eventually return to its accustomed position.

But there is another possibility. What if, for a brief moment, Maude Flanders is enjoying herself?

The unbearable predictability of goodness

The Flanders household is founded upon the elimination of uncertainty. Meals, prayers, recreational activities and interpersonal relationships are regulated by a moral system intended to make the world comprehensible. Even leisure appears to require theological approval.

The attraction of the Leftorium is that it represents something genuinely unpredictable. Ned's ceremonial burning of his pharmaceutical tie is a surprisingly dramatic gesture from a man whose usual relationship with clothing is characterised by an almost military consistency. Maude applauds. She is not visibly frightened by the prospect of failure.

The most revealing aspect of her behaviour is not that she supports her husband, but that she appears to welcome the risk itself.

There is a familiar tradition in British domestic comedy of characters who secretly long for their own routines to be disrupted. In The Good Life, the abandonment of conventional employment produces a suburban experiment in alternative living. Its comedy depends upon the collision between the security of middle-class existence and the exhilarating possibility of making an absolute mess of it.

Maude is not a suburban revolutionary. Nevertheless, the transformation of her home into a car temporarily releases her from the obligations associated with a conventional household. There are no carpets to maintain, no furniture to arrange and considerably fewer opportunities for Homer Simpson to borrow anything.

There are, however, marshmallows.

The moral complexity of a chicken bone

Maude's insistence that Homer and Ned pull a wishbone is a tiny but significant contradiction.

Here is a woman whose household is supposedly governed by an unwavering belief in divine providence, encouraging two men to participate in a ritual in which the fracture of an animal's skeleton determines the fulfilment of a wish.

It is a peculiar little piece of domestic paganism.

Such contradictions are embedded in ordinary religious life. People who would reject elaborate systems of superstition routinely participate in birthday wishes, lucky objects and rituals whose origins have become detached from their contemporary meaning. The chicken wishbone survives because nobody considers it sufficiently important to interrogate.

For Maude, its appeal is even simpler. It is fun.

That small declaration matters because pleasure rarely seems to exist independently within her world. Activities are wholesome, educational, charitable or spiritually beneficial. Enjoyment is generally the consequence of doing something appropriate rather than a sufficient justification in itself.

The wishbone offers pleasure without purpose. It also introduces chance into a household that professes to distrust gambling while staking its financial future on a shop selling left-handed merchandise.

Homer, naturally, manages to turn this innocent activity into an instrument of cruelty. Yet Maude's enthusiasm precedes his intervention. For a moment, she is the person encouraging everybody else to loosen up.

The economics of neighbourliness

Homer's acquisition of the Flanders possessions is one of the more unpleasant expressions of his relationship with Ned. He is not merely pleased by his neighbour's financial failure. He wishes to convert that failure into a material advantage.

The personalised T-shirts are particularly uncomfortable objects. Their sentimental declarations, compromised by misspelled names, become costumes in a performance of humiliation.

This is the territory of Steptoe and Son: possessions whose monetary value cannot be separated from the emotional lives of the people who previously owned them. A bargain can simultaneously be an act of theft, even when everybody has technically consented to the transaction.

The most extraordinary purchase is Ned's Bible, acquired for seven cents.

A book that may have contained Maude's underlined passages, Ned's private consolations and years of accumulated domestic significance is reduced to a negligible exchange value. Homer has acquired the physical object without acquiring any understanding of what made it valuable.

Yet Maude's cheerfulness suggests that the spiritual life of the household has survived the liquidation of its religious merchandise.

There is something rather admirable in this. The Flanders family has lost the apparatus of goodness without losing its capacity to be good.

Mary Bailey's smile

The conclusion of When Flanders Failed borrows openly from It's a Wonderful Life, including Maude's appearance and gestures.

Frank Capra's film is frequently remembered as a celebration of individual worth, but its resolution is also a rejection of the idea that financial failure necessarily constitutes moral failure. George Bailey's salvation arrives through the accumulated goodwill of a community whose existence cannot be adequately represented on a balance sheet.

Ned's rescue operates in much the same way.

The interesting difference is that Maude has already demonstrated a remarkable capacity for happiness before the money arrives. Her joy at the restoration of the business does not invalidate her earlier composure. It reveals that she is capable of inhabiting both circumstances.

Perhaps she is protecting her children. Perhaps she is exercising an unusually durable form of faith. Perhaps she genuinely enjoyed the marshmallows.

Whatever the explanation, the episode allows Maude a rare kind of emotional independence. Her happiness is not entirely contingent upon the financial success of her husband.

The Leftorium may have been Ned's great adventure.

For Maude, the more interesting adventure might have been its failure.
