The Story So Far: The Maudecast Universe

The Story So Far

The Maude Flanders Internet Museum, Theme Park and Database is operated by brothers King Brendan and King Nathan of the Cockney Watchdogs. Their acquisition of the institution is attributed both to Nathan's victory in the annual Wigan Maude-and-kick competition and to the provisions of their grandfather's will.

The Museum's collection includes self-wobbling pens, customised action figures and a life-sized Egyptian Sphinx bearing Maude's face. The latter has rendered the car park unusable. Commercial activities have also been compromised by Nathan's decision to clean the premises using the Museum's £12.99 Maude-headed mops, provoking considerable agitation from Stamp, the nightwatchman.

The theme park has recently introduced a wind chime aeroplane ride featuring miniature spectral Maudes. Meanwhile, the curators' research programme continues to expand, incorporating biographical reconstruction, artistic appreciation, audience correspondence and the retrospective correction of previous discoveries through A Week Too Late.

The Museum faces competition from a rival enterprise dedicated to Flawed Manders. Nevertheless, its audience is growing, and its activities have attracted the attention of Vivacious, who has recommended greater preparation and concision.

This Episode

A more serious institution

With the completion of its fourth broadcast, the Museum begins considering a more scholarly direction for its research. The proposed model is Cerebus Syndrome, named after the comic book that gradually developed from a parody of sword-and-sorcery adventures into an elaborate political and philosophical narrative. The curators contemplate a similar transformation, with fewer jokes and an increasingly serious approach to Maude Flanders. Whether either curator possesses the necessary discipline to implement this policy remains questionable.

The institution also celebrates the completion of its investigation into Maude's first season of appearances. Five appearances have provided considerably more material than originally anticipated, encouraging the curators to reconsider the apparent simplicity of their subject.

Uncle Statman John

The Museum acquires an unofficial statistical correspondent in the form of the curators' uncle, Statman John. His enthusiastic telephone message announces that the inaugural broadcast has achieved three hundred YouTube views, a figure exceeding the brothers' initial expectations.

John frequently appears to be eating an extremely hot potato while delivering his statistical reports, giving his communications an unusually anxious vocal quality. Despite this apparent impediment, his figures are regarded as dependable.

The curators acknowledge that their growing audience may owe something to the international popularity of the Simpsons franchise. Nevertheless, they regard their work as a campaign to transform public perceptions of Maude, converting indifference or hostility into affection one listener at a time.

Vivacious and the professionalisation of research

The advice previously supplied by Vivacious appears to be having an effect. Two full pages of notes have been prepared for the fourth broadcast. The Museum anticipates that this increased level of preparation would meet with the Drag Race contestant's approval.

A further contribution arrives from Stevie D, who incorporates Maude into an elaborate simile during an otherwise unrelated conversation. His achievement is recognised with the award of a Maude Star, establishing another potential means by which the institution can acknowledge contributions from its supporters.

The removal of the Sphinx

Although the Museum remains closed under lockdown restrictions, the curators have finally resolved the obstruction in their car park. Using shopping trolleys obtained from Tesco, the brothers and several helpful bystanders transport the life-sized Maude Sphinx away from the premises and deposit it in the local waterway.

Unfortunately, the Sphinx creates a substantial blockage, causing water to overflow and enter nearby houses. The curators are encouraged by the absence of any immediate connection between the flooding and their institution, despite the unmistakable appearance of Maude's face upon the monument.

They anticipate that erosion will gradually remove the most incriminating features of the Sphinx. This strategy is somewhat undermined by their willingness to publicly describe the entire operation.

The Museum's commercial ambitions

The Museum's gold cups shaped like Maude's head have proved popular. A separate, less publicly advertised collection of cups modelled upon her bosom is kept beneath the counter for particular customers. Only four remain available.

The success of these products illustrates the increasingly complicated relationship between the Museum's stated mission of celebrating Maude and its willingness to commercially exploit her physical appearance.

The curators also consider whether Marilyn Goodman, their reconstructed version of Maude's mother, might eventually be sold to the manufacturers of The Simpsons as an original character.

The international expansion

The Museum's origins are further clarified. Nathan's partner in the competition through which the institution was acquired was his other brother, Kieran. The competition and the grandfather's inheritance remain two apparently simultaneous explanations for the acquisition.

The Wigan establishment is revealed to be part of an international network of Maude Flanders museums. A new branch has opened in Yamoussoukro, Côte d'Ivoire, initially operating as a modest pop-up stall. Its opening stock consists of three mugs, a plastic bag containing Maude masks and a supply of pens. The curators intend to assess local demand before expanding operations.

A Turkish branch has already been operating for several months. The possibility of interviewing the manager of the Ivorian establishment is raised.

Stamp's predicament

The Museum's eighty-two-year-old nightwatchman, Stamp, has become stranded on one of the theme park's roller coasters. A recent voice message suggests that he remains unable to extricate himself.

He has apparently been trapped for approximately a week and a half. Rather than investigating his predicament, the curators have lowered additional shutters around the Museum, further restricting access to the premises.

They express confidence that a man who has successfully negotiated eighty-two years of existence should possess sufficient resourcefulness to escape from a roller coaster. Should he fail to do so, his fate will be attributed to forces beyond their control.

An expanding artistic and scholarly archive

Nathan's artistic activities extend beyond the Museum. His Instagram project, Face Forward Simpsons, contains more than a thousand illustrations of Simpsons characters viewed directly from the front, including individuals who have never appeared in the programme but whom Nathan has rendered in its distinctive visual style.

The project has been developing for approximately eighteen months. The curators propose assembling Nathan's numerous Maude illustrations into a dedicated retrospective.

The Museum also establishes a new research category, Maude's Daddies and Mommies, examining the writers, directors and artists responsible for her development. Mark Kirkland and John Swartzwelder become subjects of particular interest, while a five-hundred-page Simpsons design guide shared in part by Josh Weinstein offers the possibility of further discoveries concerning Maude's visual construction.

Correspondence, competition and cultural discoveries

An anonymous supporter expresses interest in producing an illustration of a wind chime. Another correspondent, Tom Stray Dog, submits a question by carrier pigeon concerning whether Maude is the strongest person in Springfield. Although she cannot reasonably claim superiority in physical, economic or conventional spiritual strength, the curators identify another possible category in which she might excel: persistence beyond death.

The Museum also discovers that its broadcast is difficult to locate through conventional internet searches. Among its competitors for visibility is a Filipino band called Maude, whose own video series has also used the title Maudcasts.

A separate musical discovery concerns a band called Maude Flanders, whose jazz-inflected, abrasive recording T-Shirt Cannon inspires Brendan to create an experimental audiovisual work. By synchronising the recording with slowed-down Simpsons footage, he produces a series of unexpectedly effective correspondences between music and animation. Nathan responds particularly favourably to the latter portion of the work. The possibility of contacting the musicians is raised.

Spectral Speculations

The Story So Far

Maude Goodman was born in 1954 in a farming community near Fargo, North Dakota. Her birth at home, following her parents' difficulties conceiving a child, precipitated their religious awakening. Her mother, Marilyn Goodman, raised her within a household characterised by Christian devotion and restrictions upon popular entertainment.

Maude's childhood friendship with Janice Hubler provided opportunities to secretly watch television. She also developed an interest in constructing wind chimes, completing two educational projects before producing her first independent creation at the age of fifteen: a crucifix with chimes suspended from its limbs, presented to her parents and displayed in their garden.

As an adult, Maude married Ned Flanders and became the mother of Rod and Todd. Her apparently supportive disposition was complicated by an episode of emotional withdrawal during a miniature golf tournament, when the abandonment of the competition left her staring into the distance.

Her relationship with her neighbours has also proved increasingly complicated. Homer's inappropriate behaviour towards her resulted in considerable discomfort, while a marriage counselling retreat revealed that she sometimes underlined passages in Ned's Bible. Her motivations remain uncertain, although her actions may represent an indirect attempt to address her husband's underlying anger. Despite these difficulties, her confidence in Ned remains substantial.

This Episode

The education of Rod and Todd

The precise ages of Maude's children remain disputed. One account places Rod in the same age group as Bart Simpson, while another suggests that Todd is the ten-year-old child. Their educational arrangements are similarly difficult to establish.

Although both boys have occasionally been seen at Springfield Elementary School, their regular attendance there is uncertain. Given the religious character of the Flanders household, the possibility that Maude and Ned educate their children at home warrants consideration. Alternatively, the boys may attend another of Springfield's elementary schools.

Maude's own age remains unresolved. Her apparent proximity in age to Homer and Marge suggests that she may be considerably younger than Ned. The existing biographical chronology has not yet been revised.

The first supporter

During Marge Simpson's campaign against violence in children's cartoons, Maude becomes one of the earliest individuals to express an interest in her neighbour's activities. Encountering Marge outside the television studios, she listens attentively to an explanation of the campaign and responds with calm expressions of encouragement.

Her initial involvement appears to be entirely voluntary. Ned is not visibly directing her activities, and her willingness to approach Marge suggests that she possesses independent interests in matters of public morality.

When Bart and Lisa prepare to leave, Maude accepts their explanations with a polite smile. Lisa's intention to watch television at her friend Janey's house possesses a particular resonance with Maude's own childhood experiences. Her friendship with Janice Hubler once provided precisely this kind of opportunity to circumvent parental restrictions upon television viewing.

Maude's smile may therefore contain an element of recognition. Although she supports Marge's campaign, she may also retain an affectionate memory of the small deceptions through which she obtained access to forbidden entertainment.

A different kind of protester

As Marge's campaign gathers momentum, Maude participates alongside Ned. Her placard reads “Save the Cartoon Animals,” distinguishing her contribution from the more aggressive demands of several other demonstrators.

Where other participants advocate destruction or punishment, Maude initially expresses her concerns through the language of protection. Her involvement appears consistent with her established preference for supportive rather than confrontational behaviour.

She becomes difficult to locate as the crowd expands. However, her apparent presence towards the front of the gathering suggests that she remains committed to the demonstration rather than abandoning it before its conclusion.

This early stage of the campaign presents Maude as a potentially constructive participant in Springfield's public life. Her subsequent behaviour, however, reveals a considerably less accommodating aspect of her personality.

The influence of Helen Lovejoy

Maude and Helen Lovejoy approach Marge with a proposal to protest against the exhibition of Michelangelo's David. Marge's refusal to condemn the sculpture provokes a marked change in Maude's behaviour.

Her declaration that she had already warned Helen about Marge's tolerance of nudity reveals that the two women have discussed their neighbour's moral shortcomings before approaching her. Maude's previous role as Marge's supporter has developed into something approaching a position of moral superiority.

Her expression becomes particularly hostile. The gentle attentiveness that characterised her earlier participation in the campaign is replaced by a sustained, penetrating glare.

Maude's association with Helen may be significant. Although she is capable of acting independently of Ned, her behaviour in Helen's company suggests that she is particularly susceptible to the encouragement of another morally assertive individual.

The pleasure of indignation

At the airport, Maude participates in a demonstration against the arrival of Michelangelo's sculpture. Her placard depicts the statue with its genitalia obscured and carries the slogan “No Nudes Is Good Nudes.”

While Helen expresses her hostility verbally, Maude stands glaring at the arriving Italian representative. Her apparent anger contrasts sharply with the language of protection employed during the earlier demonstration.

The transformation raises the possibility that public indignation provides Maude with an emotional outlet. Her domestic existence ordinarily requires considerable restraint, and her earlier periods of apparent emotional withdrawal suggest that this restraint may come at a cost.

Participation in a morally sanctioned protest allows her to express hostility without abandoning her conception of herself as a good Christian. What began as an act of neighbourly support has become an opportunity to exercise authority over the behaviour and beliefs of others.

A visit to Springfield Mall

Maude is subsequently seen at Springfield Mall with Rod and Todd. The three are travelling on an escalator immediately ahead of Mr Burns and Smithers.

Upon overhearing Mr Burns discussing the purchase of an exceptionally impressive present, Maude and her children turn towards him in almost perfect synchronisation. Maude appears pleased by what she hears, perhaps recognising an unexpectedly generous aspect of a man whose reputation within Springfield is considerably less favourable.

Her presence at the shopping centre also establishes a previously uncertain connection between Maude and the Springfield Mall shopping bag included with a customised action figure.

More significantly, the scene presents another example of Maude undertaking activities without Ned. Her relationship with her children possesses its own distinctive rhythm, and their synchronised movements suggest a particularly close degree of familiarity.

The prospect of financial reward

When Abraham Simpson announces his intention to distribute a substantial sum of money, Maude and Ned join the queue outside the retirement home.

Their expressions differ considerably. Ned smiles towards the television camera, while Maude looks intently ahead. Her concentration recalls the intensity with which she has previously approached competitive or morally significant situations.

The couple's intentions regarding the money remain unknown. Ned's established generosity suggests that he might intend to use it for charitable purposes. Maude's expression leaves open the possibility that she possesses a more personal interest in obtaining the money.

Her participation in the queue is not in itself evidence of greed. Nevertheless, it introduces another situation in which her motivations cannot automatically be assumed to coincide with those of her husband.

The emergence of an independent personality

Across her first five appearances, Maude has demonstrated considerable emotional and behavioural variety. She is an attentive wife, a supportive mother, a considerate neighbour and an enthusiastic participant in community affairs. She has also displayed hostility, emotional withdrawal, moral intolerance and a potentially competitive disposition.

Her relationships with Homer and Marge have already become sources of tension. Her marriage, although apparently secure, contains disagreements concerning personal boundaries and religious practice.

The influence of Helen Lovejoy introduces a further complication. Maude's capacity for independent action does not necessarily produce greater tolerance or generosity. In certain circumstances, her independence appears to facilitate precisely the opposite.

Maude Flanders is emerging as a woman whose outward gentleness conceals a considerably more complicated emotional existence.

Literary Essay

The Pleasure of Being Right

Maude Flanders, moral enthusiasm and the intoxicating possibilities of public indignation.

There is a considerable difference between wanting to save cartoon animals and wanting to prevent an Italian man from bringing a piece of marble into the country. Both activities can apparently be accommodated within the moral universe of Maude Flanders.

The distance between these two positions is not merely the distance between two protest placards. It represents the discovery of a particular kind of pleasure: the pleasure of possessing an unquestionable justification for being unpleasant.

Maude's initial involvement in Marge Simpson's campaign against cartoon violence is almost touching. She encounters her neighbour, listens patiently and offers encouragement. Her own contribution to the demonstration, “Save the Cartoon Animals,” is considerably gentler than the various demands for destruction being circulated around her. She has somehow managed to participate in a campaign against violence without advocating violence against its perpetrators.

Yet by the time Michelangelo's David arrives in Springfield, the gentleness has evaporated. Maude has discovered that the protection of innocence offers opportunities considerably more exciting than innocence itself.

The recreational possibilities of morality

British television has long understood the recreational possibilities of moral superiority. Mary Whitehouse, the campaigning figure whose activities became inseparable from the development of television culture, possessed an extraordinary professional interest in material she considered unsuitable for public consumption. The preservation of decency required sustained exposure to indecency.

Something similar happens to Maude. The woman whose childhood was characterised by restrictions upon television now finds herself participating in public discussions about violent cartoons and male genitalia.

There is an almost entrepreneurial quality to this transformation. Moral objection provides access to subjects that would otherwise be unavailable to her. A woman who might struggle to justify an afternoon spent contemplating a naked man can devote considerable time and energy to contemplating the unacceptable nakedness of Michelangelo's sculpture.

The distinction is not necessarily hypocritical. Maude may genuinely believe that public nudity is harmful. What makes her interesting is that the sincerity of her convictions does not exclude the possibility that she enjoys exercising them.

Her anger at the airport appears considerably more animated than her response to many of the supposedly pleasurable activities of suburban existence.

Perhaps indignation is Maude's hobby.

The terrible friendship

Helen Lovejoy is an important development in the emotional life of Maude Flanders.

Ned's presence ordinarily encourages Maude to perform the duties of a considerate Christian wife. Helen's presence encourages a different performance: that of the vigilant Christian citizen, alert to the moral deficiencies of other people.

Their relationship recalls the suburban alliances of Keeping Up Appearances, in which the presence of another person can transform a private eccentricity into a sustained social campaign. Hyacinth Bucket's ambitions require witnesses. Without an audience, the distinction between her imagined status and her actual circumstances would become considerably less entertaining.

Maude and Helen offer each other something similarly valuable. Each provides confirmation that the other's prejudices are not merely personal preferences but evidence of a superior moral understanding.

The most revealing moment in their encounter with Marge is Maude's admission that she had anticipated Marge's response. Before the confrontation has even taken place, Marge has already been discussed and found wanting.

The visit is therefore not entirely an invitation. It is an examination whose result Maude believes she already knows.

Her former ally has become the object of her scrutiny.

The girl who watched television

The existence of Janice Hubler makes Maude's participation in the anti-cartoon campaign considerably more interesting.

As a child, Maude circumvented her parents' restrictions by watching television at a friend's house. As an adult, she participates in a campaign intended to regulate the television available to other children.

This is a familiar generational manoeuvre. The small freedoms that make childhood bearable are frequently reclassified as dangers when the children enjoying them belong to somebody else.

Yet Maude's smile as Lisa prepares to leave for Janey's house complicates the apparent hypocrisy. There may be a residue of affection for her younger self, a recognition that childhood requires spaces in which adult supervision is temporarily suspended.

The woman campaigning against cartoons may still understand the appeal of forbidden television.

That contradiction is more compelling than a straightforward portrait of a religious censor. Maude has not necessarily forgotten the pleasures of disobedience. She has simply acquired a position from which she can determine which forms of disobedience are acceptable.

The woman in the queue

Maude's appearance outside the retirement home offers a quieter version of the same problem.

Ned smiles towards the camera while his wife concentrates upon the queue ahead. There is no reason to assume that either possesses a particularly sinister financial ambition. Nevertheless, the contrast is striking.

Maude is frequently described through the qualities she shares with her husband. The two are Christians, parents, neighbours and participants in the moral life of Springfield. But their expressions occasionally suggest that they are experiencing entirely different versions of the same event.

The miniature golf tournament provides an earlier example. What ought to have been a reassuring demonstration of friendship and Christian sportsmanship leaves Maude looking strangely vacant.

At the retirement home, her concentration suggests a woman who may possess ambitions that cannot be adequately explained through her husband's generosity.

This is not evidence that Maude is secretly monstrous. It is evidence that goodness is an insufficient description of a human being.

The great achievement of her first season is the emergence of a distinction between being good and enjoying the experience of being good.

The former requires patience, generosity and considerable self-restraint. The latter offers the more immediate rewards of certainty, authority and the gratifying discovery that somebody else is wrong.

Maude Flanders appears to be developing a taste for both.
