Literary Essay

The Woman Who Went to See Aerosmith

There is something quietly extraordinary about Maude Flanders sitting in Moe's Tavern watching Aerosmith. Ned has his arm around her, a rock band is playing, and she appears to be having a perfectly good time. For most Springfield residents this would barely count as information. For Maude it is an unexpected glimpse of a life beyond the job description of Ned Flanders' wife.

Aerosmith, by the early nineties, had turned the old theatre of rock excess into a mass-market spectacle. That is what makes their appearance so useful. Maude is not being invited to renounce her religion; she is simply allowed to enjoy something that does not belong to her household's usual moral vocabulary. Her pleasure need not be hypocrisy, rebellion or even a significant revelation. It may be no more complicated than liking a band.

The supporting wife in television comedy has traditionally been required to keep a household legible while other people get the jokes. Ned's goodness is funny because it is excessive: his courtesy, his scruples and his anxieties generate comic friction. Maude's goodness is often treated as scenery. She stands beside the joke to prove it has a family. The distinction matters because it is easy to mistake a shortage of writing for a complete personality.

The dinner-table scene in Bart the Lover offers a sharper portrait. Todd repeats Homer's profanity and Maude reacts immediately. She is not an endlessly placid saint. Yet when Ned imposes the terrible punishment of no Bible stories, she asks whether he has gone too far. She can object to her son's language and object to her husband's response. That double movement is more human than either absolute strictness or limitless patience.

Ned's story about tracking down a customer who was short-changed also passes Maude by almost without acknowledgement. She turns to the practical business of feeding Todd. It is a tiny domestic joke, but a useful one. Living with a man who turns every quarter into a spiritual emergency may require an advanced ability to change the subject.

Todd's anxiety about Davey and Goliath extends the joke. A religious programme about a boy and his talking dog becomes questionable because the talking dog itself seems blasphemous. The household can scrutinise television, comic books and bumper stickers for corrupting influences, but Homer's language still travels across the garden. Springfield refuses to remain outside the walls.

The family's charitable welcome to a homeless visitor offers another version of Maude's supporting role. She smiles, participates and seems prepared to help, yet the scene gives her little independent action. Her presence is visible and her intentions largely inaccessible. Animation can reproduce that smiling figure indefinitely without ever deciding what she thinks.

None of this proves that Maude is secretly a rock rebel or a sceptic. That would merely replace one reductive portrait with another. What the scenes reveal is the inadequacy of treating her as Ned's moral duplicate. She can be angry and merciful, devout and entertained, dutiful and privately uninterested in another of Ned's stories. Sometimes a woman watches Aerosmith because she wants to watch Aerosmith.
