The Story So Far: The Maudecast Universe

The founding curators

Biological brothers Brendan and Nathan are the founding curators of the Maude Flanders Internet Museum, Theme Park and Database. Nathan is introduced as staff member number one. He has a longstanding fascination with Maude, remembers being upset by her death on television and has compiled videos of her appearances. Brendan is less familiar with The Simpsons and relies on Nathan's specialist knowledge. He proposes a structured programme of research, allocating time to each area of discussion, while Nathan favours a freer approach.

The Museum's ambitions

The curators intend to examine every episode in which Maude speaks, with an exception for a significant non-speaking appearance. They also plan to reconstruct her life between appearances. They describe their enterprise as the leading Maude Flanders podcast and invite anyone with a more successful Maude video to come forward. They contemplate expanding from two hosts to ten or fifteen, with everyone taking turns to speak.

Brendan and Nathan consider meeting weekly or fortnightly, with approximately half an hour available each week. They discuss making money from the enterprise, recruiting an internet magnate to advise on strategy and deciding how much to invest in distribution. At least fourteen episodes are envisaged, but Brendan proposes fifty. His daughter Nami is beside him during the inaugural recording; Brendan reports that she favours a run reaching triple figures. Brendan declares that the audience will not determine the length of the series.

The first acquisitions and traditions

The curators establish Maude Art, initially concentrating on work by other artists, although they are willing to create their own when necessary. Their first reviewed exhibit is Michael Baker's tattooed Maude T-shirt, which receives ten Maudes out of ten. Nathan has previously sold his own T-shirt designs through Redbubble. Brendan jokes that he and Baker have devised the entire podcast to sell Nathan a shirt.

The Museum also establishes The World Without Maude, a department concerned with Maude's responses to contemporary events. France is the first country selected. The curators agree that the feature needs further development but retain its inaugural discussion.

The brothers have an existing tradition of composing songs together, alternating lines and repeating the final line. Brendan tends to prolong the repetition until Nathan becomes annoyed. They adapt this practice for the Museum's improvised closing song, incorporating Maude's childhood television viewing, her death and their successful first meeting. The recording ends with the brothers discussing editing and agreeing to meet again.

Spectral Speculations

Maude's childhood

At seven years old, Maude lives in a strict Christian household, possibly in North Dakota. She visits her friend Janice Hubler, where she secretly watches television. Her favourite programme is an old-fashioned children's puppet show featuring a donkey. These visits offer Maude a small escape from the restrictions of her home.

The Flanders household

Maude marries Ned Flanders and becomes the mother of Rod and Todd. A devout Christian and homemaker, she takes an active interest in the moral life of her community, joining campaigns against activities she considers objectionable. Her strict upbringing may help explain the rules of the Flanders household.

Life in Springfield

The Flanders family lives next door to Homer and Marge Simpson. Maude and Marge lead contrasting domestic lives. Maude dies in Alone Again, Natura-Diddily, during the eleventh season of The Simpsons.

The world without Maude

During the COVID-19 restrictions, May Day demonstrations in France present a dilemma for Maude: her inclination towards moral protest conflicts with her respect for public-health rules. She would ultimately stay home and pray.

The Literary Essay

The Importance of Being Nobody

There is a peculiar violence in calling somebody a minor character. Nathan, introduced as staff member number one of an institution whose title promises a museum, theme park and database, receives the suggestion that Maude Flanders is minor as though it were news. His brother has inadvertently exposed the hierarchy that the entire enterprise exists to overturn. The distinction between a protagonist and an extra is perfectly intelligible to a television producer, less so to somebody who has spent years assembling Maude's appearances. In Nathan's private distribution of significance, Springfield has been badly cast. The Maudecast proposes to correct the error without changing a single frame of the original programme.

There is a precedent in Georges Perec's attempt to describe the infra-ordinary: the stuff beneath the threshold of what usually counts as an event. But the brothers' proposed undertaking has a wonderfully British strain of administrative absurdity. They establish a distinction between speaking appearances and mere presence in a crowd, with one exception already threatening the integrity of the system. This is the kind of regulation that might occupy an entire meeting of a parish council in The Vicar of Dibley. It is also a genuine methodological problem. What constitutes an appearance? Does the silent Maude belong to the archive less than the speaking one? The comedy depends on the question being simultaneously ridiculous and worth answering.

Brendan's ambition to prove something to nobody in particular is a better description of the intellectual life than many university prospectuses. He proposes a timetable, Nathan supplies the expertise, and the two contemplate the assistance of an internet magnate. The conversation repeatedly approaches the managerial language of an Alan Partridge enterprise, only to retreat into the practical question of whether either brother can spare half an hour. Their disagreement about the number of episodes is particularly revealing: an archive supposedly dedicated to exhaustive research has no reliable way of knowing its eventual size. The subject will determine the scale of the institution, rather than the other way round.

The peculiar productivity of absence

Nathan's account of Maude's death supplies a surprisingly rigorous explanation of why this institution might be necessary. A living Maude could be placed in the background of the Flanders household without explanation; a dead Maude must be brought back for a reason. The less available she becomes, the more narrative weight her appearances acquire. In Twin Peaks, Laura Palmer's absence is the engine of the drama. Here the engine has to be fitted retrospectively by two enthusiasts. The Maudecast is an attempt to produce the gravitational field around Maude that her original programme never required.

What gives this argument its emotional force is Nathan's inability to remember whether he was already devoted to Maude when she died or whether her death inaugurated that devotion. The uncertainty is more interesting than an origin myth with a tidy date. Television familiarity can accumulate unnoticed, rather like affection for the incidental sounds of a childhood home. The death of a supporting character makes that familiarity suddenly perceptible. A cartoon household that usually resets itself has undergone a permanent alteration, and the viewer discovers that he had been relying on its furniture.

It is no accident that the brothers' invented childhood returns Maude to the television set. Their reconstruction of her forbidden visits to Janice Hubler's house gives the adult moral guardian an early experience of disobedience, but the object of her rebellion is not sex, drugs or some lurid secret worthy of a prestige drama. It is a donkey puppet. The reference to Muffin the Mule, followed by the image of an almost illegible black-and-white programme in which a donkey encounters a box, makes the imagined past feel wonderfully cheap. The brothers understand something that expensive fictional universes frequently forget: a convincing childhood requires not only formative traumas but also rubbish television.

The donkey's declaration of its own donkeyhood is a tiny piece of absurdist theatre. Beckett could have appreciated the economy of the arrangement, though it belongs equally to the realm of threadbare children's broadcasting and the manufactured nostalgia of Look Around You. Maude's pleasure in such an unpromising spectacle mirrors Nathan's pleasure in Maude. Both are acts of attention bestowed upon an object that the wider culture might regard as scarcely worth looking at.

Against the tyranny of the interesting

Even the tattooed Maude on Michael Baker's T-shirt participates in this redistribution of attention. The crucifix beneath her eye becomes the basis of an elaborate interpretative manoeuvre: perhaps the mark signifies a soul saved rather than a life taken. A visual joke becomes a question about Maude's moral universe. The brothers' discussion of her response to French May Day demonstrations performs a related operation. Her enthusiasm for protest and her obedience to authority pull in different directions. The apparent simplicity of her personality proves insufficient to predict her behaviour.

These moments suggest that the Maudecast's real antagonist is not the neglect of one cartoon woman but the assumption that interesting things announce themselves as such. The archive, the merchandise, the half-hour timetable and the donkey all become instruments for testing that assumption. Even the brothers' closing musical ritual, with its deliberately overextended repetition, insists on staying with something after its conventional usefulness has expired. The Museum's first great achievement is not to demonstrate that Maude was secretly the most important character in The Simpsons. It is to make importance itself look like an increasingly inadequate reason for paying attention.
