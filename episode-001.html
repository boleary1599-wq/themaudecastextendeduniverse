The Story So Far: The Maudecast Universe

Brendan and Nathan are brothers and the founding curators of the Maude Flanders Internet Museum, Theme Park and Database. Their institution begins with an ambitious research programme: examine Maude’s appearances, discuss what happens on screen and imagine what happens between scenes.

The curators debate the scope, frequency and possible commercial future of their podcast. The Maude Art segment introduces a review of a Michael Baker T-shirt, awarded ten Maudes out of ten. The Museum is still in its formative stages.

Spectral Speculations

Series status: Episode 1 of the Maudecast. This page tracks what the podcast discusses, not everything known about The Simpsons. Claims are marked as established series background, the hosts' interpretation, or invented speculation.

The Flanders household

Maude Flanders is introduced as Ned Flanders's wife and the mother of Rod and Todd. The hosts describe her as a devout Christian, a conventional homemaker and a moral guardian who sometimes joins campaigns against things she considers objectionable. They are especially interested in how she can be the straight woman to an already exceptionally straight-laced husband. This is their reading of her role, rather than a new event in the story.

Ned Flanders is the husband around whom the household is often organised. Brendan and Nathan wonder whether Maude's upbringing, rather than Ned's, might explain the family's strict domestic culture. That remains their hypothesis, not an established fact.

Rod and Todd are identified as Maude and Ned's sons. No new event involving either boy is recounted in this instalment.

Next door, and beyond the grave

The Flanders family lives beside the Simpsons in Springfield. The hosts propose Maude as a kind of opposite or mirror to Marge Simpson, and discuss how her role sometimes becomes more conspicuous when she is separated from the rest of her family. Homer Simpson enters the discussion through the hosts' uncertain recollection of a joke involving Maude and a bowl of peanuts; the podcast does not settle that memory.

Maude's death is the major established plot event mentioned here. The hosts place it in Season 11's Alone Again, Natura-Diddily, and discuss how her death altered the way later appearances work: a character who once could simply stand in the Flanders house now needs a narrative reason to return. Nathan recalls being upset when she died and wonders whether that experience began, or strengthened, his fascination with her.

Spectral Speculations: the childhood that might have been

The first piece of podcast-created, non-canonical lore imagines seven-year-old Maude in a strict Christian household, possibly in North Dakota. She secretly watches television at a friend's house because her own family may forbid it. The friend is named Janice Hubler in the conversation. Her favourite programme is imagined as an odd, old-fashioned children's puppet show, involving a donkey. The hosts disagree about the precise decade and treat the whole scenario as conjecture. Janice is therefore an MFIMDb speculative character, not a verified Simpsons character.

Maude after death: the world without Maude

The hosts launch another recurring idea: imagining what the deceased Maude would make of current events. Their first test concerns May Day demonstrations in France during COVID-19 restrictions. They speculate that her fondness for moral protest might conflict with her instinct to obey public-health rules, and that she would ultimately stay home and pray. This is imagined character commentary, not an event in The Simpsons.

The Literary Essay

In Praise of the Minor Character

The museum of almost nothing

There is something magnificently wrong with the scale of the Maudecast. Two brothers announce themselves as curators of an internet museum, theme park and database devoted to a woman whose principal function in an extraordinarily successful television programme was often to stand next to her husband. The institutional apparatus is Versailles; the exhibit is Maude Flanders. It is the same comic disproportion that animates the Ministry of Silly Walks, where the machinery of government exists to administer an absurdly specialised activity. But Brendan and Nathan are not merely performing a sketch about obsessive fandom. They actually intend to do the research. The distinction matters: the longer the joke continues, the more useful the institution threatens to become.

Consider the proposed methodology. The brothers will examine Maude's appearances, distinguish speaking roles from incidental background appearances and speculate about what occurs between scenes. This sounds like the work of a particularly determined biblical exegete, or Vladimir Nabokov's Charles Kinbote in Pale Fire, whose commentary threatens to overwhelm the poem it ostensibly serves. It also sounds like an ordinary afternoon on a fan wiki. The internet has democratised the footnote to the point where a minor cartoon character can acquire the documentary infrastructure once reserved for a Roman emperor. Maudtroductions asks what happens when that infrastructure is inhabited by two people who know perfectly well how ridiculous it is.

The advantages of being dead

The most penetrating observation in the episode concerns Maude's death. Before it, the hosts suggest, she could simply be present in the Flanders household. Afterwards, her appearance requires justification. Death, normally the termination of a character's narrative possibilities, becomes a guarantee of narrative significance. It is an argument about television grammar disguised as a conversation between two blokes about a cartoon.

Laura Palmer in Twin Peaks is the obvious grand comparison: a dead woman becomes the organising principle of an entire community's story. Maude is almost the inverse. Her death does not turn Springfield into a vast investigation of her secret life; the Maudecast has to build that investigation itself. The brothers effectively establish a private Twin Peaks around a character whose original programme has other business to attend to.

Nathan's recollection of being upset by her death introduces an emotional complication. The affection precedes, or perhaps follows from, the loss; he cannot quite establish which. Anyone who has mourned the disappearance of a favourite supporting character from a soap opera understands this peculiar attachment. The supposedly disposable inhabitants of television become part of the furniture of our lives. Nobody needs to explain why a chair matters until somebody takes it away.

The donkey and the forbidden television

Spectral Speculations is where the Museum crosses from criticism into fiction. The imagined young Maude, secretly watching television at a friend's house, is a splendidly modest act of rebellion. No secret cult, no spectacular crime, no prestige-television revelation that explains everything. Instead, the brothers conjure a primitive children's puppet programme featuring a donkey and a box. The speculative biography acquires the texture of an obscure regional television broadcast nobody can quite remember.

It is tempting to invoke Borges and his invented encyclopaedias, but the more immediate cultural relatives are the fabricated television memories of Look Around You and the aggressively unglamorous puppet worlds of British children's broadcasting. The donkey is funny because the brothers expend imaginative energy on the quality of the entertainment available to a child who does not exist. They are not simply supplying Maude with a backstory; they are supplying her with a media environment. This is world-building conducted at the level of a tatty puppet rather than a map of Middle-earth.

The episode's other counterfactual, Maude's imagined response to pandemic-era demonstrations in France, extends the experiment into politics. Her instinct for moral protest collides with her respect for rules. A character initially described through a handful of conservative traits becomes interesting precisely when those traits cease to produce an obvious answer.

Ten Maudes out of ten

The Michael Baker T-shirt reviewed in Maude Art offers a miniature version of the entire project. A tattooed Maude sitting on a Bible is an image built from the friction between an established character and an incompatible visual vocabulary. The hosts linger over the crucifix beneath her eye, imagining a religious alternative to the conventional meaning of a teardrop tattoo. Even merchandise becomes an opportunity for speculative theology.

There is a touch of Alan Partridge in the brothers' discussion of audience size, commercial prospects and their own expertise, and something of the institutional deadpan of Garth Marenghi's Darkplace in the disproportion between their ambitions and their resources. Yet the affection for the subject prevents the enterprise from collapsing into a straightforward parody of incompetence. Their enthusiasm is the real thing. The Museum can be ridiculous without its exhibits being worthless.

The opening episode therefore establishes a productive contradiction: an archive devoted to preserving a fictional woman's history must continually invent material to fill its shelves. The Maudecast is both the curator and the forger, the fan wiki and the fan fiction. Its greatest discovery may be that the distinction between a minor character and a major one is partly a question of who is prepared to keep looking.
