The Story So Far: The Maudecast Universe

The founding curators

Biological brothers Brendan and Nathan are the founding curators of the Maude Flanders Internet Museum, Theme Park and Database. Nathan is introduced as staff member number one. He has a longstanding fascination with Maude, remembers being upset by her death on television and has compiled videos of her appearances. Brendan is less familiar with The Simpsons and relies on Nathan's specialist knowledge. He proposes a structured programme of research, allocating time to each area of discussion, while Nathan favours a freer approach.

The Museum's ambitions

The curators intend to examine every episode in which Maude speaks, with an exception for a significant non-speaking appearance. They also plan to reconstruct her life between appearances. They describe their enterprise as the leading Maude Flanders podcast and invite anyone with a more successful Maude video to come forward. They contemplate expanding from two hosts to ten or fifteen, with everyone taking turns to speak.

Brendan and Nathan consider meeting weekly or fortnightly, with approximately half an hour available each week. They discuss making money from the enterprise, recruiting an internet magnate to advise on strategy and deciding how much to invest in distribution. At least fourteen episodes are envisaged, but Brendan proposes fifty. His daughter Nami is beside him during the inaugural recording; Brendan reports that she favours a run reaching triple figures. Brendan declares that the audience will not determine the length of the series.

The first acquisitions and traditions

The curators establish Maude Art, initially concentrating on work by other artists, although they are willing to create their own when necessary. Their first reviewed exhibit is Michael Baker's tattooed Maude T-shirt, which receives ten Maudes out of ten. Nathan has previously sold his own T-shirt designs through Redbubble. Brendan jokes that he and Baker have devised the entire podcast to sell Nathan a shirt.

The Museum also establishes The World Without Maude, a department concerned with Maude's responses to contemporary events. France is the first country selected. The curators agree that the feature needs further development but retain its inaugural discussion.

The brothers have an existing tradition of composing songs together, alternating lines and repeating the final line. Brendan tends to prolong the repetition until Nathan becomes annoyed. They adapt this practice for the Museum's improvised closing song, incorporating Maude's childhood television viewing, her death and their successful first meeting. The recording ends with the brothers discussing editing and agreeing to meet again.

Spectral Speculations

Maude's childhood

At seven years old, Maude lives in a strict Christian household, possibly in North Dakota. She visits her friend Janice Hubler, where she secretly watches television. Her favourite programme is an old-fashioned children's puppet show featuring a donkey. These visits offer Maude a small escape from the restrictions of her home.

The Flanders household

Maude marries Ned Flanders and becomes the mother of Rod and Todd. A devout Christian and homemaker, she takes an active interest in the moral life of her community, joining campaigns against activities she considers objectionable. Her strict upbringing may help explain the rules of the Flanders household.

Life in Springfield

The Flanders family lives next door to Homer and Marge Simpson. Maude and Marge lead contrasting domestic lives. Maude dies in Alone Again, Natura-Diddily, during the eleventh season of The Simpsons.

The world without Maude

During the COVID-19 restrictions, May Day demonstrations in France present a dilemma for Maude: her inclination towards moral protest conflicts with her respect for public-health rules. She would ultimately stay home and pray.

The Literary Essay

The Importance of Being Nobody

There is a peculiar violence in calling somebody a minor character. The designation makes perfect sense as a production category, but becomes considerably less satisfactory as a description of a life. Maude Flanders is a supporting character to Ned Flanders, himself a supporting character in The Simpsons. She is the straight woman to the straight man: a figure whose dramatic function is to make an already conspicuously ordinary person appear more interesting. The Maude Flanders Internet Museum, Theme Park and Database reverses this hierarchy. Springfield becomes the supporting infrastructure of Maude's existence.

Georges Perec's interest in the infra-ordinary offers a useful precedent. What happens beneath the threshold of what usually counts as an event? A speaking appearance is relatively easy to catalogue, but a silent figure in a crowd poses a more interesting problem. The distinction between significant and insignificant appearances is already unstable before the archive has properly begun. There is something of the Ministry of Silly Walks in the disproportion between the institutional machinery and its subject, but also a serious challenge to the conventions by which cultural significance is allocated. Why should a character's allotted dialogue determine the limits of her biography?

The Flanders household is particularly susceptible to this kind of excavation because it presents itself as a completed ideological object. The churchgoing, cleaning and praying evoke an idealised American nuclear family of the 1950s, preserved in the amber of late twentieth-century animation. Maude's apparent function as the anti-Marge depends on the assumption that domestic conformity is an absence of personality. Yet the moral guardian who emerges most forcefully when separated from her husband and children suggests something rather different. Respectability is not necessarily passivity. It can be a means of exercising power.

The peculiar productivity of absence

Death produces a remarkable alteration in the grammar of Maude's existence. Before it, she could occupy the background of the Flanders household without explanation. Afterwards, any appearance requires a narrative justification. A character becomes more significant precisely because she can no longer be casually present. Laura Palmer in Twin Peaks is the grand example of absence becoming the organising principle of a fictional world. Maude's death achieves something comparable on a much smaller scale: the formerly incidental figure acquires the peculiar privilege of being summoned rather than simply encountered.

This is especially disruptive in a cartoon whose domestic arrangements generally survive the events of each episode. Familiarity accumulates unnoticed, rather like affection for the incidental sounds of a childhood home. The permanent removal of a supporting character exposes an attachment that may never previously have been articulated. It is difficult to establish whether the affection precedes the loss or is produced by it. Either way, the furniture of an apparently indestructible fictional household has suddenly become irreplaceable.

The reconstruction of Maude's childhood offers an appropriately modest response to that absence. At seven, in a strict Christian household somewhere around Fargo, television becomes forbidden territory. Janice Hubler's house provides access to an alternative world. The object of this small rebellion is not some lurid secret worthy of a prestige television drama, but an almost illegible children's puppet programme. Muffin the Mule supplies a cultural point of reference; the imagined entertainment itself consists of a donkey moving up and down, encountering a box and declaring its donkeyhood.

Beckett might have appreciated the economy of the arrangement, although it belongs equally to the threadbare aesthetics of early children's broadcasting and the manufactured nostalgia of Look Around You. The programme is important because it is so unimportant. A convincing fictional childhood requires not only formative traumas but also rubbish television. Maude's delight in an unpromising puppet is an act of attention as disproportionate, and as inexplicable, as the devotion her own cartoon existence inspires.

Against the tyranny of the interesting

Michael Baker's tattooed Maude, seated on a Bible in pink underwear, complicates the relationship between rebellion and respectability. The crucifix beneath her eye invites a reversal of the conventional symbolism associated with a teardrop tattoo: a soul saved rather than a life taken. The image need not signify the abandonment of her religious identity. It might instead represent its translation into an incompatible visual vocabulary. The supposedly transgressive body remains an instrument of Christian conviction.

A similar contradiction emerges in the imagined response to French May Day demonstrations during the pandemic. The moral protester confronts a conflict between the impulse to demonstrate and the obligation to obey. Staying indoors and praying for the demonstrators resolves the immediate dilemma without eliminating the tension. The conservative personality is not a single instruction from which every action can be deduced. It is a collection of commitments capable of contradicting one another.

Between the forbidden donkey, the tattooed crucifix and the deserted Flanders household lies a common proposition: interesting things do not necessarily announce themselves as such. The minor character is not an unfinished major character waiting for a promotion. Her marginality creates possibilities that the heavily documented protagonist cannot enjoy. The gaps in Maude's biography are not merely deficiencies to be repaired; they are spaces in which a different kind of imaginative life can occur. The point of a museum devoted to Maude Flanders is not to prove that she was secretly the most important character in The Simpsons. It is to make importance itself look like an increasingly inadequate reason for paying attention.
