The Story So Far: The Maudecast Universe

The Story So Far

The Maude Flanders Internet Museum, Theme Park and Database is staffed by brothers Brendan and Nathan, whose shared ambition is to document the existence of Maude Flanders in unprecedented detail. Nathan, the institution's longest-serving employee, possesses considerable specialist knowledge, while Brendan, a more recent recruit, oversees the organisation of their research and broadcasts.

The Museum's activities extend beyond conventional archival work. Its collection includes artwork, merchandise and Egyptian-themed Simpsons memorabilia. A delivery of self-wobbling pens and a life-sized Maude Flanders Sphinx has temporarily rendered the car park unusable.

Meanwhile, a rival institution of sorts has emerged in the form of a podcast dedicated to Flawed Manders, Maude's evil twin. The curators have established several research traditions, including the reconstruction of Maude's undocumented past, the appraisal of Maude-related artwork and the improvisation of songs incorporating their latest discoveries. Their mother remains a somewhat reluctant supporter, having listened to approximately half of the inaugural broadcast before becoming bored, although she has promoted their work on Facebook.

This Episode

The mysterious acquisition of the Museum

The Museum is situated just off Worsley Mesnes, a useful detail for visitors intending to arrive by road. Its curators, King Nathan and King Brendan of the Cockney Watchdogs, possess an unusual claim to the property. Nathan and his competition partner originally acquired the institution through the annual Wigan Maude-and-kick competition, an event combining the rapid illustration of Maude Flanders with the repeated kicking of a football against a wall. Nathan drew the greatest number of Maudes within sixty seconds, while his partner excelled at the football component. The competition traditionally awards its winners a large abandoned museum.

The acquisition became considerably more mysterious when it subsequently transpired that the Museum had also been left to the brothers in their grandfather's will. Brendan was still engaged in cleaning the park when Nathan first took possession of the institution. The precise relationship between the competition and the inheritance remains unexplained.

Stamp and the destruction of the merchandise

Stamp, the Museum's nightwatchman, has become embroiled in a dispute concerning the institution's merchandise. An agitated voice message left for Nathan concerns the Maude Flanders mops, retailing at £12.99 apiece. Each mop consists of a representation of Maude's head attached to an otherwise conventional mop handle. Nathan has appropriated a substantial proportion of the stock to clean the premises, apparently dissatisfied with the standard of cleanliness maintained by Stamp.

Stamp's responsibilities, however, do not extend to cleaning. The curators regard him as a subordinate whose authority to question their decisions is extremely limited. Although Nathan has not used every mop for cleaning, even the remaining examples are reportedly unsuitable for sale. The incident exposes an emerging tension between the Museum's commercial ambitions, its maintenance requirements and the distribution of responsibilities among its employees.

The wind chime aeroplane ride

The Museum's theme park contains sufficient attractions for its curators to have developed personal favourites. Among the newest installations is the wind chime aeroplane ride, which allows visitors to experience the movement and sounds of a wind chime while travelling upwards, downwards and around in circles. Small spectral Maude Flanders figures fly around the riders' heads, appearing and disappearing throughout the experience.

The attraction represents a particularly rapid conversion of the Museum's research discoveries into commercial entertainment. Maude's previously overlooked interest in constructing wind chimes has already become part of the institution's physical infrastructure.

A growing audience and an expanding research programme

The Museum introduces a new recurring feature, A Week Too Late, dedicated to discoveries made after the completion of previous broadcasts. Its initial investigations concern the relationship between Dead Putting Society and Dead Poets Society, the contributions of writers and directors to Maude's gradual development, the rules governing Simpsons character design and the conflicting reported ages of Rod and Todd Flanders.

The broadcasts have accumulated more than two hundred views, although this achievement remains modest beside Nathan's earlier Maude Flanders video compilation, one example of which has attracted approximately half a million views.

Messages of encouragement arrive from a contributor apparently based in Japan and from Mr S. Aspinall, who particularly appreciates the detailed examination of Maude's facial expressions during the miniature golf tournament. Winnie submits a question concerning Maude's ability to change a tyre. The Museum also receives encouragement from Vivacious, a contestant from the sixth season of RuPaul's Drag Race, who recommends preparing notes and exercising concision. The latter recommendation may prove incompatible with the institution's increasingly expansive research philosophy.

The proposed Maude Art feature concerning a necklace is postponed, allowing additional time for biographical research. Plans are established for the next broadcast to examine Maude's remaining speaking appearances in the second season and assess her development across the season as a whole.

Spectral Speculations

The Story So Far

Maude Goodman was born in 1954 in a farming community near Fargo, North Dakota. Her arrival, following her parents' belief that they might never have children, precipitated their religious awakening. Marilyn Goodman gave birth at home, assisted by a midwife, on a pleasantly grey day. Maude's childhood was shaped by Christianity and domestic restrictions.

Her friendship with Janice Hubler provided opportunities to secretly watch television, offering a small escape from the constraints of her upbringing. She subsequently married Ned Flanders and became the mother of Rod and Todd, establishing a household characterised by religious devotion, domestic order and consideration for others.

Among her less widely known accomplishments is the construction of homemade wind chimes. During the Springfield miniature golf tournament, Maude's commitment to peaceful resolution appeared to undergo an unexpected crisis. When Bart and Todd abandoned their competition in favour of a draw, she remained motionless, staring into the distance. Whether this represented disappointment, emotional exhaustion or a more fundamental collapse of her worldview remains unresolved.

This Episode

The third wind chime

At the age of fifteen, Maude Goodman completed her third wind chime. Her first two constructions had been undertaken for educational purposes, but the third represented her first independent exploration of the craft for personal pleasure. Still living with her parents in North Dakota, Maude conceived the object as a gift for them, combining her emerging artistic interests with the religious convictions that dominated her adolescence.

The wind chime took the form of a crucifix, with small tinkling chimes suspended from the limbs of Jesus Christ. Although the combination of crucifixion imagery and decorative garden ornamentation might have appeared somewhat questionable to others, Maude's intentions were entirely sincere. Her craftsmanship was sufficiently accomplished for the figure to be recognisable, although the finished object was unlikely to win an artistic competition.

The wind chime was hung near the entrance to the family garden, where it tinkled gently in the North Dakotan spring breeze. The garden was not the elaborate, enclosed sanctuary of The Secret Garden, but a considerably more modest domestic space.

The woman in the red dress

Years later, Maude attends a gathering at the Simpsons' home wearing a striking red dress and a pearl necklace, a considerable departure from the lime-green Sunday outfit she wore during the miniature golf tournament. She arrives smiling broadly and initially converses with Marge while their husbands move elsewhere.

At one point, Maude is conspicuously absent from the assembled guests. Her whereabouts are unknown, although she may have been assisting with preparations in the kitchen. Her established domestic competence makes this plausible, but there is no confirmation.

Homer, increasingly intoxicated, requests that Maude fetch him some peanuts. By directing her towards a particular bowl, he engineers an opportunity to look down her dress. Maude initially responds with characteristic politeness, apparently uncertain whether she has misunderstood his request. Marge witnesses the incident with considerable displeasure.

Homer's subsequent recollection of the evening depicts Maude reacting with horror while the Lovejoys, Hibberts and Ned Flanders gather around him. The precise relationship between this recollection and the actual events remains uncertain.

The limits of neighbourliness

The following morning, Maude encounters Homer at church. Wearing her familiar combination of a pink top and open-necked shirt, she recoils from him and fastens her upper button as he passes. Whatever uncertainty she may previously have entertained regarding his intentions, she is now sufficiently uncomfortable in his presence to take defensive action.

Outside the church, however, Maude appears composed, conversing pleasantly with a woman accompanied by two small children in large white hats. The woman may be Linda, although her identity remains unconfirmed. Maude's apparent composure contrasts with the activities of her husband, who is inside arranging for them to attend a marriage counselling retreat.

It remains unclear whether Maude was aware of Ned's intentions beforehand. Their participation might initially have appeared to be another opportunity for Ned to spend time in the company of Reverend Lovejoy, but the retreat subsequently reveals a genuine domestic disagreement.

The annotated Bible

At the marriage retreat, Maude initially maintains her customary attentive posture, sitting with her hands upon her lap. When Reverend Lovejoy invites the Flanders couple to explain their presence, Maude encourages Ned to speak. His grievance is that she sometimes underlines passages in his Bible when she cannot locate her own.

The revelation raises several questions concerning Maude's private life. Her reputation for domestic order makes the repeated disappearance of her Bible particularly curious. It is possible that the underlining represents an indirect attempt to communicate her own emotional difficulties. Alternatively, Maude may be deliberately drawing Ned's attention to passages that she believes will help him overcome his underlying anger and become a better Christian.

The latter possibility is consistent with her established inclination to resolve difficulties through helping others. Her apparent shame during Ned's disclosure may therefore arise not from the act itself, but from the unexpected discovery that her efforts have caused him distress.

Maude experiences further humiliation when Homer openly acknowledges his behaviour at the party. She gasps and raises her hands towards her face, displaying a degree of emotional agitation rarely seen during her earlier appearances.

Sleep, trust and reconciliation

During Marge's prolonged account of her marital difficulties, Maude falls asleep. Although the events under discussion have directly affected her, she appears unable or unwilling to maintain her interest in the Simpsons' domestic problems. Her exhaustion may reflect the accumulated strain of recent events or simply a diminishing tolerance for the behaviour of her neighbours.

Nevertheless, the retreat concludes with a striking demonstration of her confidence in Ned. During a trust exercise, Maude closes her eyes and falls backwards into her husband's arms without hesitation. Marge, whose husband has already departed, is unable to complete the same exercise.

Maude and Ned appear to leave the retreat with their relationship intact. Whether she continues to annotate his Bible remains unknown, although her respect for his wishes suggests that she may seek alternative methods of guiding him towards spiritual improvement.

An unresolved chronology

Maude's age remains uncertain. Conflicting accounts of Rod and Todd's ages, together with Ned's reported age and the unusual passage of time in Springfield, complicate the established chronology of her life. The possibility that Maude is considerably younger than her husband remains open. Her established birth year of 1954 has not yet been formally revised.

Literary Essay

The Subversive Art of Underlining

On religious handicrafts, marital ownership and the quiet rebellion of Maude Flanders.

There is something magnificently inappropriate about a crucifix that doubles as a wind chime. The central image of Christian suffering, reproduced in countless churches and bedrooms as an invitation to solemn contemplation, becomes a garden ornament that makes a pleasant noise whenever the weather changes. Somewhere between the devotional object and the merchandise department of a garden centre lies the artistic territory occupied by the fifteen-year-old Maude Goodman.

Her third wind chime is her first genuinely independent artistic undertaking. The previous two belong to the world of education, where creativity is performed according to instructions and evaluated by an authority. The third belongs to Maude. Its peculiar combination of religious sincerity and aesthetic misjudgement suggests a teenager beginning to discover the possibilities of personal expression without possessing the cultural vocabulary necessary to distinguish rebellion from obedience.

It is precisely the sort of object that might appear in a John Waters film, although Maude lacks Waters' awareness of the relationship between bad taste and liberation. Her wind chime is not knowingly kitsch. Its creator genuinely believes that the crucified Christ might benefit from the addition of several small bells.

The result is an accidental act of irreverence by someone attempting to be reverent.

Private property

Years later, Maude commits a similar offence against another religious object. She underlines passages in her husband's Bible.

The distinction between this act and the construction of the wind chime is that the Bible belongs to Ned. Maude's teenage artwork represents an imaginative intervention into the iconography of Christianity; her adult annotations constitute an intervention into somebody else's relationship with it.

The comedy of the marriage counselling scene depends upon the apparent triviality of Ned's grievance. Alongside the considerable catalogue of Homer Simpson's indiscretions, a woman underlining passages in a book appears almost heroically insignificant. Yet Ned's distress is real. His mounting agitation suggests that the ownership of the Bible matters almost as much as its contents.

There is an echo here of the suburban territorial disputes in Ever Decreasing Circles, in which Martin Bryce's determination to maintain order repeatedly exposes the emotional fragility beneath his domestic authority. The disturbance of a familiar object becomes intolerable because the object belongs to a system through which its owner understands himself.

What makes Maude's intervention particularly interesting is that she may be using the very source of Ned's authority to challenge him. If her intention is to encourage greater patience, humility or compassion, she is not opposing his religious worldview. She is attempting to redirect it.

This is dissent conducted in the language of obedience.

The question of why Maude repeatedly loses her own Bible is consequently more interesting than the underlining itself. In a household apparently maintained with extraordinary care, the disappearance of a regularly consulted book seems suspiciously convenient.

Perhaps the book is not really lost.

The woman who wears the pearls

The red dress Maude wears to the Simpsons' party presents another small disturbance in Springfield's established visual order.

The pearl necklace is particularly interesting because of its association with Marge. For once, the familiar signifier of suburban femininity has migrated to the neck of another woman. Maude becomes an alternative version of the wife Homer already possesses, sufficiently familiar to be desirable and sufficiently different to constitute a fantasy.

The situation recalls the elaborate domestic substitutions of Alfred Hitchcock's Vertigo, although Homer lacks the obsessive aesthetic sophistication of James Stewart's Scottie Ferguson. His imagination extends little further than the manipulation of a bowl of peanuts.

The scene is uncomfortable because Maude's politeness becomes the mechanism through which Homer exploits her. Her willingness to accommodate a minor request exposes her to an indignity that a less considerate person might have avoided.

By the following morning, she has learnt to fasten her shirt.

This small defensive gesture is more revealing than any grand declaration of hostility. Maude does not publicly confront Homer or abandon the social conventions of neighbourliness. Instead, she modifies her behaviour to accommodate the existence of a man who cannot be trusted.

The cost of maintaining Springfield's social harmony is disproportionately borne by the people who are least responsible for its disruption.

The luxury of falling asleep

Maude's decision, conscious or otherwise, to fall asleep during Marge's account of her marital problems is one of the most satisfying developments in her early existence.

The sitcom neighbour traditionally performs an extraordinary amount of unpaid emotional labour. From the residents of The Good Life to the unfortunate inhabitants of the streets surrounding the central families of American domestic comedies, proximity to the protagonist constitutes an obligation to participate in their difficulties.

Maude briefly withdraws that participation.

Her sleep might represent exhaustion, boredom or a simple refusal by her subconscious to continue absorbing the consequences of Homer Simpson's behaviour. Whatever its origin, it provides a rare interval during which she is unavailable to other people.

There is something quietly liberating about the image of Maude Flanders asleep while the central characters of The Simpsons continue explaining themselves.

Yet her subsequent trust fall into Ned's arms complicates any straightforward reading of her as a woman imprisoned by domesticity. Her confidence in her husband appears absolute. She can experience disappointment in him, attempt to improve him and still believe that he will catch her.

Perhaps the underlined Bible is not evidence of a failing marriage but of an unusually intimate disagreement about what constitutes goodness.

The teenage Maude who attached bells to a crucifix has become the adult Maude who places lines beneath the words of God. In both instances, she takes something apparently complete and introduces a modest alteration of her own.

Neither gesture is revolutionary in the conventional sense. Both suggest that somewhere beneath the reassuring surface of Maude Flanders exists a woman who believes that even sacred things might benefit from a little revision.

The Mauditorium

Here are five of today's most significant discoveries about Maude.

  • Fashion. Maude wears a red dress and pearls to the Simpsons' party, demonstrating a wardrobe beyond her familiar church clothes.
  • Boundaries. After Homer's inappropriate behaviour at the party, Maude fastens her shirt when he passes her at church, revealing her discomfort and willingness to protect her boundaries.
  • Scripture. Maude underlines passages in Ned's Bible when she cannot find her own. Whether this reflects disorganisation or an indirect attempt to guide him remains uncertain.
  • Patience. Maude falls asleep during Marge's account of her marital problems, suggesting that her patience with the Simpsons may have limits.
  • Trust. Maude falls backwards into Ned's arms during a trust exercise without hesitation, demonstrating confidence in him despite their disagreement.

Elsewhere in the Mauditorium. Her whereabouts during part of the party and the identity of a woman she speaks to outside church remain uncertain. The invented biography gives her a crucifix-shaped third wind chime at fifteen; her age chronology is unresolved.
